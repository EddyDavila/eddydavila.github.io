Environment direction / spatial design / gameplay iteration

A place to dine.
A world to explore.

Aurora is a VR restaurant-world prototype connected to my Augmented Taste project. I direct the experience, environment and gameplay decisions, with AI-assisted engineering.

My focus is the transition from a seated, readable interaction space into an environment with routes, landmarks and a short competitive activity. This is independent development—not a claim that I managed a studio team or shipped an open-world title.

01 / Layout and visual communication

Give the player something to read.

A clear point of interest

The pavilion anchors the scene. Its entrance, stair approach and lit arches establish a destination against the much larger mountain backdrop. The distant panorama provides atmosphere; it is not advertised as traversable world geometry.

Routes with visible boundaries

The paved promenade contrasts with the planting beds. Lamps repeat along the route, while the curved path offers changing views of the central structure. The layout supports movement around a focal space rather than a corridor ending at a menu.

02 / Playability and interaction

Separate the calm space from the challenge.

The prototype supports seated interaction, virtual exploration and a return-to-seat flow. Movement checks cover supported ground, jumping, platform boundaries and preventing upward head direction from unintentionally adding altitude while walking. Controller input and the ordering presentation remain distinct systems.

A later local multiplayer test adds a moving target to the environment. Players hold a condiment bottle, aim and fire at the target. The host owns scoring and the round deadline; only the winner receives a flight reward. The reward changes how the same space can be explored.

Evidence boundary: existing headset captures establish the environment's appearance. Automated movement and network tests establish specific rule checks. They do not establish sustained headset performance, two-human-headset acceptance or production multiplayer readiness.

03 / Difficulty and pacing

Pressure comes from movement and pursuit.

A moving, reactive target

The latest local test uses collider-validated route links. The target travels at 1.25 m/s and, within seven metres of a recently tracked player, selects branches that increase separation. Escape speed is capped at 2.2 m/s. It can reverse on an existing link without teleporting.

A bounded competitive loop

A three-minute round creates a clear endpoint. Confirmed hits determine the winner; tied leaders enter sudden death. A hit briefly hides the target before it respawns and resumes movement. These are implemented prototype rules, not a claim that difficulty is fully player-balanced.

Next validation: observe whether players understand the target's escape behavior, whether pursuit routes remain readable, and whether the reward is comfortable and satisfying across repeated headset sessions. Real multi-headset latency and production anti-cheat remain open work.

04 / Technical constraints and honest authorship

Art direction with implementation limits in mind.

  • The approved pavilion lighting bakes fixture contribution into static mesh vertex colors; fixture Light components are disabled at runtime. This is not Unity lightmapping or a claim of dynamic shadows.
  • The local host validates target hits, sequence and revision; route generation checks scene colliders. Production authentication, recovery and multi-headset testing remain separate gates.
  • I own the creative direction and production decisions. Engineering is AI-assisted; this portfolio does not claim that every asset was modeled or painted by hand.
  • The VR prototype reuses the Augmented Taste ordering core rather than presenting a separate, unverified payment system as finished work.

Selected evidence: Quest VIP prototype handoff; approved lighting baseline; October 1 shared-game test results; local host documentation. Private project records and credentials are not published.
